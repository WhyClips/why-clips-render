In 1971, NASA flew five hundred tree seeds around the Moon — and the trees they grew into still stand in America today, one of them at the White House.

Apollo 14 launched in January 1971. While two astronauts walked on the Moon, Stuart Roosa circled alone above them with five hundred seeds in a tiny canister. A former forest firefighter, he took them to see what space does to living things.

But the seeds never touched the Moon — they looped around it thirty-four times while astronauts took the glory. Then splashdown — the canisters burst, and the seeds spilled out, mixed like spilled salt. Everyone thought it was over.

They planted them anyway, in Mississippi and California — and almost every one sprouted: four hundred and twenty seedlings, stubborn as weeds after a wildfire.

For America's two hundredth birthday, the baby trees went to every state. First planting: Philadelphia, May nineteen seventy-five. A pine went to the White House — others to Brazil and Japan's Emperor.

Then the trees were forgotten, until a schoolteacher asked NASA where they were. One scientist hunted them down from old newspaper clippings — a hundred and ten found. You see, the seeds everyone wrote off as dead became the longest-living souvenirs of the Moon landings. And in twenty twenty-two, NASA did it again — Artemis One carried a thousand new seeds around the Moon, their grandchildren growing right now, maybe in a park near you.
