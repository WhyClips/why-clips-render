A man who couldn't read or write dreamed a whole alphabet — and by morning, his language could be written.
1833, Liberia: Momolu Duwalu Bukele can't read a single word.
One night, a stranger in a dream teaches him signs for every sound.
He wakes — the signs are gone. Forgotten. But the idea stays.
So he gathers his family. In a day and a night, they invent about 200 symbols — one for every syllable of Vai.
Outsiders laugh: writing comes from empires, schools, centuries — not from a dream.
Some say he may have glimpsed another alphabet first — but nobody disputes he built this one himself.
Within a generation, Vai people write letters, diaries — a script no European taught them. They crush berries for ink, build their own schools.
The idea catches fire — inspiring at least 27 other West African scripts.
You see, writing wasn't handed to Africa. It was dreamed there.
It survives colonial rule, two rival scripts, 200 years. It's taught at the University of Liberia — and in Unicode, so people text in it on their phones.
And the man who started it all never learned to read a word anyone else had written.
